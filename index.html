VAT Registration Threshold 2026: When Must a UK Business Register for VAT?

The registration threshold is £90,000 of taxable turnover — measured on a rolling 12 months, not a tax year, and not your profit. Here is how the test actually works, with a worked example and the deregistration threshold most guides forget.

The rule, in one paragraph

The test: at the end of every month, add up your taxable turnover for the previous 12 months. If the total passes £90,000, you must register for VAT — generally from the first day of the second month after you crossed it. Sales that are exempt from VAT do not count toward the total; zero-rated sales do.

Two habits make the test manageable. First, run the rolling total monthly, as part of closing the books — a business that checks once a year can discover the obligation months late, and HMRC charges for the VAT that should have been collected in between. Second, count taxable turnover (everything you sell that isn't VAT-exempt), not your bank balance and not your profit.

The second test: the next 30 days

Crossing £90,000 is not the only trigger. You must also register if you expect your taxable turnover in the next 30 days alone to pass £90,000 — one large contract can do it. In that case registration is needed from the date you realised the expectation, and VAT applies from that date, not from when the paperwork catches up.

A worked example: crossing the threshold in month 11

A growing design studio bills steadily, then lands a larger client. Watching the rolling 12-month total — not the calendar year — is what catches the crossing:

MonthTaxable salesRolling 12-month total
Months 1–6 (total)£44,400£44,400
Month 7£8,200£52,600
Month 8£8,600£61,200
Month 9£9,000£70,200
Month 10£9,500£79,700
Month 11£10,400£90,100 — threshold crossed
Month 12£10,600£100,700 (registered by now — VAT charged from the effective date)

The studio crossed £90,000 during month 11 — even though no single month, and no tax year viewed alone, looked dramatic. That is exactly the pattern the rolling test exists to catch, and exactly why monthly checking matters. Once registered, the studio charges VAT on its invoices and reclaims VAT on its costs; pricing for any consumer customers needs a decision, because a price that included no VAT yesterday must carry it tomorrow.

Coming back out: the £88,000 deregistration threshold

If your taxable turnover falls and you expect it to stay below £88,000 for the next 12 months, you can ask HMRC to cancel your registration. Businesses often deregister after losing a large contract or changing direction. Until the cancellation is confirmed, you keep charging and filing as normal.

Voluntary registration, in brief

  • For it: you reclaim VAT on purchases — valuable if your costs carry a lot of VAT, or your customers are VAT-registered businesses who reclaim whatever you charge.
  • Against it: if your customers are the public, registering usually means either raising prices by 20% or absorbing the VAT yourself; plus returns and digital record-keeping from day one.
  • Registering voluntarily and then choosing a scheme (standard, cash accounting, or the flat rate scheme) is a numbers decision — run your own last four quarters before you choose.
